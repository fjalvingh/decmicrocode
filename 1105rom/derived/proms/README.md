# KD11-B control-store PROM images

Ten 256x4 PROMs per revision, assembled from the transcribed microcode listing. **These are
derived data, not dumps** — nobody read them off a chip. They are what the listing says the
chips should contain.

Generated by `../ocr/promgen.py`, verified by `../ocr/promcheck.py`.

## Read this before burning anything

Two things about the physical encoding are **not known** from the sources, and neither can be
settled from the listing. Both are single switches at the top of `promgen.py`.

1. **Address polarity.** The images are in *true microaddress* order: `image[a]` holds the
   microword whose `LOC` is `a`. But the microaddress signals are `MPC-0-L` … `MPC-7-L`, active
   low — the same fact that makes the `NXT` field a complement — so if the microaddress latch
   drives the PROM address pins directly, the device may want `image[~a]` instead. The µPC-to-PROM
   wiring has not been read off the schematic. Set `COMPLEMENT_ADDRESS = True` to flip it.
2. **Which schematic bit reaches which data output.** The images take the lowest schematic bit of
   a slice as `D0`. The notes give the bit range per PROM but not the pin order. Set
   `MSB_FIRST = True` to flip it.

Get either wrong and the images are still 256 plausible-looking bytes. **Check both against a
board before programming a part.** Reading one original PROM and diffing it against the matching
image here settles both questions at once, and would turn this from derived data into verified
data.

## The 42 holes

The listing prints 214 of the 256 control-store locations. The other 42 are filled with `F`,
which on a fusible-link part means *leave unprogrammed*. That value is also harmless if the
machine ever reaches one: an all-ones microword has no bus cycle, no branch, every control line
inactive, and an unconditional next address of `000` — `RS-1`, the reset entry.

The unlisted addresses, in octal:

```
002 044 054 055 056 066 073 076 102 114 120 121 122 133 150 161 170 171 175 202 233
242 243 266 267 271 275 277 306 323 327 336 344 345 356 360 362 363 366 370 376 377
```

## What this confirms

**Eight of the ten PROMs keep their part number between rev E and rev F, and all eight come out
byte-identical.** The two that change part number are the only two that differ. That was derived
here from the transcribed bits alone, and it agrees with the part numbers read off the schematics
— two independent routes to the same answer, and the generator asserts it rather than reporting
it, so it cannot drift.

The differences land exactly where the bit map says they should:

| PROM | bits | locations differing | change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `23A14A2` → `23A19A2` | 8–11 | 6 | `7` → `F`, i.e. bit 11 (`CKOFF-L`, the top of the nibble) 0 → 1 |
| `23A15A2` → `23A20A2` | 24–27 | 14 | `3`→`2`, `B`→`A`, `E`→`F`, i.e. bit 24 (`AUX-CONTROL-L`, the bottom of the nibble) flipping |

Six locations differ in *both* PROMs — `154`, `352`, `353`, `354`, `355`, `373`, which are `MB-0`
and `U1-1` … `U5-1`. Those are the microwords where `AUX` and `CKO` changed together, across two
different chips.

## Verification

`promcheck.py` reassembles all 40 bits of every microword from the ten images and compares against
the listing. **214/214 exact for both revisions**, and all ten images read `F` at each of the 42
unlisted locations. That is the project's round-trip rule applied: a writer and a reader tested in
one place, so a wrong slice map cannot hide.

What is *not* verified is anything physical — the two conventions above, and whether a real chip
matches. See the warning at the top.
